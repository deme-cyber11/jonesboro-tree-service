Tree Trimming vs Pruning: What's the Difference?

An expert guide to understanding when your trees need trimming, pruning, or both.

  1. Home
  2. Blog
  3. Tree Trimming vs Pruning
January 5, 2025 6 min read Tree Care

Homeowners often use the words "trimming" and "pruning" interchangeably when talking about tree care. While both involve cutting branches, they serve fundamentally different purposes and require different techniques. Understanding the difference helps you communicate more effectively with your tree service provider and ensures your trees get the right care at the right time.

What Is Tree Trimming?

Tree trimming is primarily about maintaining appearance and managing growth. When you trim a tree, the goal is to shape the canopy, control its size, and keep it looking neat. Think of trimming as the cosmetic side of tree care.

Common trimming tasks include:

  • Shaping the canopy to maintain a balanced, attractive form
  • Cutting back overgrowth that blocks views, sunlight, or walkways
  • Reducing the overall size of a tree that has grown too large for its space
  • Maintaining clearance from rooflines, gutters, power lines, and fences
  • Keeping hedges and ornamental trees in a desired shape

Trimming is typically done on the outer portion of the canopy, where new growth is densest. It focuses on the tips and ends of branches rather than major structural limbs. For hedges, shrubs, and small ornamental trees, trimming may be the primary form of maintenance you need.

What Is Tree Pruning?

Tree pruning is a more targeted, health-focused practice. When you prune a tree, the goal is to improve its structural integrity, remove problem branches, promote healthy growth, and reduce safety hazards. Pruning is closer to tree surgery than tree grooming.

Common pruning tasks include:

  • Removing dead, diseased, or dying branches (deadwooding)
  • Eliminating crossing or rubbing branches that can create wounds and entry points for disease
  • Correcting structural problems like co-dominant leaders (two trunks competing for dominance)
  • Opening up the canopy to allow better air circulation and light penetration
  • Removing water sprouts and suckers that drain the tree's energy
  • Raising the canopy by removing lower branches for clearance underneath

Pruning requires a deeper understanding of tree biology. Each cut must be made at the right location, specifically just outside the branch collar, to allow the tree to heal properly. Improper pruning cuts, such as flush cuts or stub cuts, can cause lasting damage.

Types of Professional Pruning

The arboriculture industry recognizes several distinct types of pruning, each with specific objectives. At Jonesboro Tree Pros, we follow ANSI A300 standards, which are the nationally accepted guidelines for tree care operations in the United States.

Crown Thinning

Crown thinning involves selectively removing branches throughout the canopy to reduce density. This allows more sunlight and air to pass through, which reduces wind resistance during storms and promotes healthier inner growth. The goal is to maintain the tree's natural shape while opening it up. A properly thinned canopy should not look noticeably different from the ground; it should just feel lighter and more open.

According to ANSI A300 standards, no more than 25% of the live crown should be removed in a single pruning session. Removing too much foliage at once stresses the tree and triggers excessive regrowth (called "epicormic sprouting"), which is weak and defeats the purpose of thinning.

Crown Raising

Crown raising, also called "limbing up," involves removing the lower branches of a tree to provide clearance. This is commonly done to allow pedestrian or vehicle traffic underneath, to clear sight lines, or to let more sunlight reach the lawn or garden below.

For mature trees, the live crown should never be raised above the lower third of the tree's total height. Removing too many lower branches shifts the tree's center of gravity upward and can make it top-heavy and unstable.

Crown Reduction

Crown reduction decreases the overall height or spread of a tree's canopy by cutting branches back to a lateral branch that is at least one-third the diameter of the branch being removed. This is different from "topping," which is the destructive practice of indiscriminately cutting branches back to stubs.

Topping is never acceptable. It destroys the tree's natural form, creates weak regrowth that is more dangerous than the original branches, and leaves large wounds that invite decay and disease. Crown reduction, done correctly, maintains the tree's structural integrity while managing its size.

Crown Cleaning

Crown cleaning is the removal of dead, dying, diseased, and broken branches from the canopy. This is the most basic and universally beneficial type of pruning. Every tree benefits from having its deadwood removed regularly, as dead branches are unpredictable falling hazards and can harbor pests and disease.

The Best Season for Trimming and Pruning in Jonesboro

Timing matters when it comes to tree work. The best time depends on what you are trying to accomplish and the species of tree involved.

Late Winter to Early Spring (February - March)

This is generally the best time for major pruning work on most deciduous trees in the Jonesboro area. The trees are dormant, the branch structure is visible without leaves, and the tree will begin healing as soon as spring growth starts. Oaks, maples, elms, and most hardwoods do well with late winter pruning.

Summer (June - August)

Summer pruning is useful for controlling growth and shaping. Because the tree is actively growing, summer pruning has a dwarfing effect, meaning it slows growth more than dormant-season pruning. This is the ideal time for maintenance trimming, removing water sprouts, and managing canopy size. Deadwood removal can be done any time of year.

Seasons to Avoid

Fall (September - November) is generally the worst time to prune. Decay fungi spread their spores most aggressively in fall, and fresh pruning cuts provide easy entry points. Trees are also entering dormancy and are less able to compartmentalize wounds.

Spring during active growth (April - May) should also be approached with caution. Trees are expending significant energy producing new leaves and shoots. Heavy pruning during this period can stress the tree. Additionally, oaks should not be pruned from April through July in areas where oak wilt is a concern, as the beetles that spread the disease are most active during this period.

ANSI A300 Standards: Why They Matter

The American National Standards Institute (ANSI) A300 standards provide science-based guidelines for tree care practices. When you hire a tree service that follows ANSI A300, you can expect:

  • Proper pruning cuts made just outside the branch collar, allowing the tree to seal the wound naturally
  • No topping. ANSI A300 explicitly prohibits topping as a pruning practice
  • Appropriate removal percentages. No more than 25% of the live canopy removed in a single session
  • Species-appropriate timing. Pruning schedules that account for each tree species' growth patterns and vulnerabilities
  • Clear communication. A written scope of work specifying the type of pruning to be performed

Not every tree company follows ANSI A300. When getting quotes for tree trimming or pruning, ask the company if they adhere to these standards. Companies employing ISA-certified arborists are trained in ANSI A300 practices and are your best assurance of quality work.

Why Professional Service Matters

While you can safely trim small ornamental trees and shrubs yourself, pruning mature trees should always be left to professionals. Here is why:

  • Safety. Working at height with cutting tools is inherently dangerous. Professional arborists have the training, equipment, and safety protocols to do the job without injury.
  • Tree biology knowledge. Knowing where to cut, how much to remove, and when to do it requires training in arboriculture. A bad pruning cut can cause damage that takes years to manifest.
  • Proper equipment. Professional pruning requires hand saws, pole pruners, chainsaws, and sometimes aerial lift trucks. Using the right tool for each cut produces clean wounds that heal faster.
  • Long-term tree health. A tree that is properly pruned throughout its life will be stronger, healthier, and safer than one that receives sporadic or improper care.
  • Liability protection. A licensed and insured tree service protects you from liability if something goes wrong during the work.
