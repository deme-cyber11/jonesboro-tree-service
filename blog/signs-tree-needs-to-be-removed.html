7 Signs a Tree Needs to Be Removed

How to tell when a tree on your property has become a safety hazard and needs to come down.

  1. Home
  2. /
  3. Blog
  4. /
  5. Signs a Tree Needs Removal

Trees add beauty, shade, and value to your property, but they do not last forever. When a tree becomes diseased, structurally compromised, or dangerously positioned, it can pose a serious risk to your home, your family, and your neighbors. Knowing when a tree has crossed the line from asset to liability is critical. Here are seven warning signs that indicate a tree on your property may need to be removed by a professional.

1. More Than 50% of the Canopy Is Dead

Every tree drops a few dead branches from time to time, and that is normal. But when more than half of the canopy is dead, bare, or failing to produce leaves during the growing season, the tree is in serious decline. A tree with over 50% dead canopy has lost the ability to sustain itself through photosynthesis and is unlikely to recover, regardless of treatment.

Dead branches are also a direct safety hazard. They can fall without warning, especially during wind, rain, or ice events. In Jonesboro, where spring and summer storms are common, a tree with extensive deadwood overhead is a liability you should not ignore. If you notice large sections of bare branches during a time when the tree should be leafy and green, call an arborist for an assessment. In many cases, removal is the safest and most cost-effective option compared to repeated deadwood pruning on a dying tree.

2. Hollow or Severely Cracked Trunk

The trunk is the structural backbone of a tree. When the trunk develops deep cracks, large cavities, or becomes hollow, it loses the structural integrity needed to support the weight of the canopy. A cracked or hollow trunk can fail suddenly and without warning, sending the entire tree crashing down.

It is important to understand that a tree can appear alive and green on the outside while being hollow on the inside. The living tissue of a tree (the cambium layer) is just beneath the bark, so leaves can continue to grow even after the heartwood has rotted away. If you can stick your hand into a cavity in the trunk, or if you notice vertical splits running along the length of the trunk, the tree needs a professional evaluation. A certified arborist can assess how much structural wood remains and determine whether the tree is safe to keep standing or needs to come down.

3. Leaning Toward a Structure

Some trees naturally grow at a slight angle, and that is not necessarily a problem. But if a tree that previously stood upright has developed a noticeable lean, or if it is leaning toward your house, garage, power lines, or a neighbor's property, that is a different situation entirely. A new or worsening lean indicates that the root system is failing or the soil is shifting, and the tree could fall in the direction of the lean.

Pay particular attention to the soil around the base of the tree. If you see raised or cracked soil on the side opposite the lean, that means the roots are pulling out of the ground. This is a sign of imminent failure. A tree leaning toward a structure should be evaluated immediately by a professional. In most cases, removal is the only safe course of action, especially if the lean has developed suddenly after a storm or period of heavy rain.

4. Root Damage or Heaving

The root system is the foundation of a tree, and when roots are damaged, the entire tree is compromised. Root damage can be caused by construction activity, soil compaction, grading changes, prolonged flooding, or disease. Signs of root problems include visible root decay, mushrooms growing at the base of the tree, and soil heaving (the ground around the base lifting or cracking).

In the Jonesboro area, root damage from construction is particularly common. When new driveways, sidewalks, or additions are built close to established trees, the root zone is often disturbed by excavation, trenching, or soil compaction from heavy equipment. The damage may not become apparent for one to three years, but once the roots fail, the tree becomes unstable. If you have had recent construction near a large tree and notice declining health or soil movement around the base, schedule a tree health assessment before the situation becomes dangerous.

5. Fungal Growth at the Base

Mushrooms, conks (shelf-like fungal growths), or other fungi growing at the base of a tree or on the lower trunk are a strong indicator of internal decay. Fungi feed on dead and decaying wood, so their presence tells you that the wood inside the tree is rotting. The more extensive the fungal growth, the more advanced the internal decay is likely to be.

Not all fungi are equally alarming. Some surface mushrooms near a tree are growing on decaying organic matter in the soil, not on the tree itself. But bracket fungi (also known as conks) that grow directly from the trunk or root flare are almost always a sign of serious internal rot. In northeast Arkansas, common culprits include ganoderma (artist's conk), sulfur shelf fungus, and hen-of-the-woods. If you see any of these growing on or at the base of your tree, have it inspected. An arborist may use a resistograph or other diagnostic tool to determine the extent of internal decay and whether the tree can be safely retained.

6. Major Storm Damage

Severe storms are a fact of life in Jonesboro and Craighead County. Spring and summer bring thunderstorms, straight-line winds, and occasional tornadoes that can devastate trees. After a major storm, a tree may suffer damage that makes it unsafe to keep, even if it is still standing.

Signs of storm damage that warrant removal include the loss of more than one-third of the canopy from broken limbs, a split trunk or major scaffold branch failure, bark stripped from large sections of the trunk, and root plate lifting (when one side of the root system heaves out of the ground). A tree that has lost a major portion of its structure is unlikely to regain its former shape and will remain a hazard for years. Trees with split trunks are especially dangerous because the remaining halves can fall at any time. If a tree on your property has sustained major storm damage, do not attempt to assess or clean up the situation yourself. Call a licensed tree service for an emergency evaluation.

7. Invasive Root System Damaging Your Foundation

Some tree species have aggressive root systems that can cause serious damage to foundations, driveways, sidewalks, sewer lines, and underground utilities. Willows, silver maples, sweetgums, and certain oaks are among the worst offenders. When roots from these species grow under or against a foundation, they can crack concrete, shift footings, and create moisture pathways that lead to basement leaks.

If you are noticing cracks in your foundation, heaving sidewalks, or recurring sewer backups, and there is a large tree within 20 feet of the affected area, the root system may be the cause. A tree that is actively damaging your home's foundation is not one you can afford to keep, regardless of how much you value the shade it provides. Removing the tree and grinding the stump eliminates the root growth and allows you to address the structural damage without the roots continuing to push against the repair. In some cases, a root barrier can be installed as an alternative to removal, but this only works when the damage is minor and the tree is healthy enough to tolerate significant root pruning.

When in Doubt, Call a Professional

Not every tree with a crack, dead branch, or fungal growth needs to be removed. Trees are resilient, and many conditions can be managed through pruning, cabling, or treatment. The key is getting an honest, expert evaluation from a certified arborist who will give you a straightforward recommendation rather than pushing unnecessary work.
