7 Signs Your Tree Needs to Be Removed

Know the warning signs before a hazardous tree becomes an emergency.

  1. Home
  2. Blog
  3. 7 Signs Your Tree Needs to Be Removed
January 20, 2025 7 min read Tree Health

Trees add beauty, shade, and property value to your home, but they do not last forever. Age, disease, storm damage, and structural problems can turn a once-healthy tree into a serious liability. The challenge is knowing when a tree has crossed the line from manageable problem to genuine hazard.

As ISA-certified arborists serving Jonesboro and Craighead County, we evaluate trees every week that homeowners are unsure about. Some can be saved with treatment or pruning. Others need to come down before they cause damage or injury. Here are the seven most reliable signs that your tree needs professional removal.

1. Dead or Dying Branches Throughout the Crown

Every tree drops the occasional dead branch, and that is normal. What is not normal is a tree with dead branches scattered throughout its canopy, especially in the upper crown. This pattern, sometimes called "dieback," indicates that the tree is declining from the inside out.

What to look for:

  • Bare branches when the rest of the tree is leafed out
  • Branches with brown, shriveled leaves while others are green
  • Large dead limbs overhead (often called "widow makers")
  • Bark falling off branches, revealing smooth, dry wood underneath

If more than 25% to 30% of the crown is dead, the tree is unlikely to recover and should be evaluated for removal. Dead branches also pose an immediate falling hazard, especially during Arkansas windstorms.

2. Trunk Damage and Decay

The trunk is the structural backbone of your tree. When it is compromised, the entire tree becomes unstable. Trunk damage can come from equipment strikes (like lawnmowers), lightning, ice storms, or internal decay from fungi and bacteria.

What to look for:

  • Vertical cracks or splits running up the trunk
  • Large areas of missing bark (called "cankers")
  • Soft, spongy, or crumbling wood when you press on the trunk
  • Mushrooms or fungal conks growing on or at the base of the trunk
  • Carpenter ants or other wood-boring insects emerging from the trunk

Fungal growth on a tree trunk is especially concerning. Species like sulfur shelf fungus, artist's conk, and honey mushrooms indicate active internal wood decay. By the time fungi appear on the outside, significant internal damage has already occurred.

3. Significant Leaning

Some trees grow at an angle naturally, and that is fine. The concern is when a tree has recently started leaning or when an existing lean has gotten worse. A sudden lean usually means the root system has failed on one side, and the tree is at risk of toppling over completely.

What to look for:

  • A lean that was not there before, or has gotten noticeably worse
  • Cracked or heaved soil on the side opposite the lean
  • Exposed or broken roots on the lifting side
  • The tree leans toward your house, driveway, or a neighbor's property

A tree leaning more than 15 degrees from vertical is generally considered high-risk. If you notice a sudden lean after a storm, keep people and vehicles away from the fall zone and call a professional immediately.

4. Root Damage or Decay

Root problems are trickier to spot because most of the root system is underground. But there are visible signs that indicate the roots are failing, and root failure is one of the most common reasons trees fall over.

What to look for:

  • Mushrooms growing in a ring around the base of the tree
  • Soil heaving or cracking near the trunk
  • Roots that have been cut or damaged during construction, trenching, or utility work
  • The tree seems to rock or shift slightly when pushed (on a calm day)
  • Recent grade changes around the tree, such as adding fill dirt over the root zone

In Jonesboro, construction and landscaping projects frequently damage tree roots without anyone realizing the severity. A tree can survive with some root loss, but once more than 30% to 40% of the root system is compromised, the tree may not be able to support itself, especially in high winds.

5. Hollow Trunk

Trees are remarkable in that they can remain standing even with a hollow center. The outermost wood (sapwood) handles water and nutrient transport, while the inner heartwood provides structural strength. However, once the hollow reaches a critical size, the trunk no longer has enough solid wood to bear the weight of the crown.

What to look for:

  • Visible cavities or holes in the trunk
  • A hollow sound when you knock on the trunk
  • Animals nesting inside the trunk (raccoons, squirrels, owls)
  • Sawdust-like material (frass) accumulating at the base

As a general rule, if the hollow area comprises more than one-third of the trunk's circumference, the tree's structural integrity is seriously compromised. An arborist can use specialized tools to determine the exact extent of internal decay.

6. Disease or Pest Infestation

Arkansas trees are vulnerable to several diseases and pests that can weaken or kill them. Some of the most common problems we see in the Jonesboro area include:

  • Oak wilt: A fungal disease that clogs the water-conducting vessels in oak trees, causing rapid leaf drop and death. Red oaks can die within a few weeks of showing symptoms.
  • Pine bark beetles: These small beetles bore into pine trees, disrupting nutrient flow. Infested pines often show reddish-brown needles and pitch tubes on the bark.
  • Bacterial leaf scorch: A chronic disease affecting oaks, elms, and other hardwoods. Leaves develop brown, scorched margins that progressively worsen each year.
  • Hypoxylon canker: A fungal pathogen that attacks stressed hardwoods, particularly oaks. Look for dark, crusty patches on the bark.
  • Emerald ash borer: This invasive beetle has devastated ash tree populations across the eastern United States and continues to spread through Arkansas.

If a disease has progressed to the point where more than half the tree is affected, treatment may not be viable. In some cases, removing a diseased tree is also necessary to prevent the pathogen from spreading to nearby healthy trees.

7. Too Close to Structures

Sometimes the tree itself is perfectly healthy, but its location has become a problem. Trees that were planted too close to a house, garage, foundation, or driveway can cause ongoing damage as they grow.

What to look for:

  • Roots lifting sidewalks, driveways, or foundation walls
  • Branches constantly rubbing against siding, gutters, or the roof
  • The tree's canopy overhangs most of your roof
  • Roots clogging sewer lines or septic systems
  • The tree blocks sight lines at a driveway or intersection

In many cases, strategic pruning can manage the problem. But if the tree is so close that its root system is actively damaging your foundation or its trunk is within a few feet of your home, removal may be the only long-term solution.

When to Call an Arborist vs. DIY Assessment

You can do a basic visual inspection of your trees yourself using the signs listed above. Walk around each tree on your property at least once a year, ideally in late spring when problems are most visible, and look for any of these warning signs.

However, call a certified arborist when:

  • You see any of the signs above but are not sure how serious the problem is
  • A tree has changed noticeably since the last time you looked at it
  • A storm has damaged a tree and you need to know if it can be saved
  • You want an expert opinion before spending money on removal
  • The tree is large enough that falling branches could hit a person, vehicle, or structure

A professional assessment from an ISA-certified arborist gives you a clear answer and a plan. In many cases, the arborist may recommend treatment or pruning instead of removal, potentially saving you money and preserving a valuable tree.
