Storm Damage Tree Cleanup in Jonesboro, AR

What to do after a storm damages your trees, and when to call a professional for help.

  1. Home
  2. /
  3. Blog
  4. /
  5. Storm Damage Cleanup

Jonesboro sits in the heart of northeast Arkansas, an area that sees its share of severe weather. Spring and summer bring powerful thunderstorms, straight-line winds, hail, and the occasional tornado. When one of these storms tears through your neighborhood, the aftermath often includes downed trees, broken branches, and debris scattered across your yard and possibly your roof. Knowing what to do immediately after a storm, how to assess damage, and when to call a professional can save you time, money, and most importantly keep you safe.

Immediate Safety Steps After a Storm

The first priority after any severe weather event is personal safety. Trees and branches may appear stable but can be under tension, partially supported, or tangled in power lines. Before you walk your property or attempt any cleanup, follow these steps:

  • Stay away from downed power lines. If a tree or branch has fallen on or near a power line, do not approach it. Assume every downed line is live and call Craighead Electric Cooperative or your utility provider immediately. Keep children, pets, and neighbors away from the area.
  • Do not go under damaged trees. A tree that is partially fallen, leaning, or has large hanging branches (widow-makers) can shift or collapse without warning. Stay clear of the drop zone, which extends at least as far as the height of the tree in every direction.
  • Check for gas leaks. If a tree has struck your home or fallen near gas meters or lines, leave the area immediately and call your gas company before re-entering.
  • Document everything before touching anything. Take photos and video from a safe distance before any cleanup begins. This documentation is critical for insurance claims and will save you significant frustration later in the process.
  • Secure your property. If a tree has created an opening in your roof or wall, use tarps to prevent further water damage while you arrange for professional help. Do only what you can do safely from the ground.

Assessing Tree Damage: Can It Be Saved?

Not every storm-damaged tree needs to be removed. Trees are remarkably resilient, and many can recover from significant damage if they receive proper care. Here is how to evaluate whether a damaged tree is worth saving or needs to come down:

Trees That Can Usually Be Saved

  • Minor branch loss: If the tree has lost less than one-third of its canopy from broken branches but the trunk and major scaffold limbs are intact, it will likely recover with corrective pruning.
  • Small to medium branch breaks: Broken branches under 4 inches in diameter can be pruned back to proper branch collars without significantly affecting the tree's long-term health.
  • Bark damage on one side: If bark has been stripped from a section of the trunk but more than two-thirds of the trunk circumference retains intact bark, the tree can often heal over time.

Trees That Likely Need Removal

  • Split trunk: A tree that has split down the middle or lost a major co-dominant leader is structurally compromised and will remain a hazard.
  • More than 50% canopy loss: A tree that has lost more than half its crown from broken limbs will struggle to recover and may decline over the following seasons.
  • Root plate failure: If the root ball has partially lifted out of the ground and the tree is leaning, the root system has been fatally compromised. Even if the tree is re-staked upright, it will never re-establish the root anchorage needed to be safe.
  • Trunk damage: Deep cracks, large wounds exposing heartwood, or significant sections of missing bark around the trunk indicate the tree will be prone to decay and future failure.

If you are unsure whether a tree can be saved, call a certified arborist for a post-storm assessment. Attempting to make this determination yourself can lead to costly mistakes in either direction: removing a tree that could have been saved, or keeping one that eventually fails and causes damage.

When to Call a Professional vs. DIY

After a storm, it is tempting to grab a chainsaw and start cleaning up yourself. While homeowners can safely handle some minor debris cleanup, there are clear lines that should not be crossed without professional help:

Safe for Homeowners

  • Picking up small branches and debris that are already on the ground
  • Raking leaves and small twigs from the lawn
  • Cutting branches under 3 inches in diameter that are on the ground with no tension

Call a Professional

  • Any tree or branch that is in contact with or near power lines
  • Hanging branches or limbs still attached to the tree (these are under tension and can snap unpredictably)
  • Trees leaning against structures, fences, or other trees
  • Any chainsaw work above shoulder height
  • Fallen trees that are supporting other trees or structures (weight transfer creates dangerous instability)
  • Large limbs or trunk sections that require rigging to remove safely

Storm damage cleanup is one of the most dangerous activities in the tree care industry. Twisted, leaning, and partially broken trees behave unpredictably. Professional tree crews have the training, equipment, and insurance to handle these situations safely. It is not worth risking a serious injury to save a few hundred dollars.

Insurance Claims for Storm-Damaged Trees

Understanding how insurance handles storm damage to trees can save you thousands of dollars. Here is what most Jonesboro homeowners need to know:

What Homeowners Insurance Typically Covers

  • Tree removal when a tree falls on a structure: If a tree falls on your house, garage, shed, fence, or other insured structure, your homeowners policy will generally cover the cost of removing the tree and repairing the structure, minus your deductible.
  • Tree removal blocking access: If a fallen tree is blocking your driveway or a paved walkway, most policies cover removal even if no structure was damaged.
  • Debris removal: Many policies include a debris removal provision that covers cleanup costs associated with a covered peril (storm damage).

What Insurance Typically Does NOT Cover

  • Tree falls in yard but hits nothing: If a tree falls in your yard and does not strike any insured structure, most policies will not cover the removal cost. The tree is considered your responsibility as a property maintenance issue.
  • Preventive removal: Insurance does not pay to remove a tree before it falls, even if it is obviously hazardous. Preventive removal is a maintenance expense.
  • Replacement landscaping: The cost of replacing trees, shrubs, or landscaping damaged by a storm is typically not covered, or is subject to very low limits.

Tips for Filing a Claim

  • Document all damage with photos and video before any cleanup begins.
  • Contact your insurance company as soon as possible to open a claim.
  • Get a written estimate from a licensed tree service before authorizing work. Your insurer may require multiple estimates.
  • Keep all receipts for emergency repairs, temporary measures (tarps, board-up), and tree removal.
  • Do not sign contracts with door-to-door storm chasers who appear after severe weather. Work with established local companies.

Jonesboro's Storm Season: What to Expect

Northeast Arkansas experiences severe weather primarily from March through August, with peak storm activity in April, May, and June. Craighead County sits in an area that sees an above-average number of severe thunderstorm events annually, including supercell thunderstorms capable of producing large hail, damaging winds exceeding 60 mph, and tornadoes.

The combination of Jonesboro's mature tree canopy, clay-heavy soils that become saturated during prolonged rain, and the frequency of severe wind events means that storm damage to trees is not a matter of if, but when. Proactive tree maintenance, including regular trimming, deadwood removal, and health assessments, is the best way to minimize damage before a storm arrives. Trees that are well-maintained with proper structure and no dead limbs fare significantly better in severe weather than neglected ones.

We recommend scheduling a tree health assessment in late winter or early spring, before storm season begins, so any hazards can be identified and addressed while conditions are calm and scheduling is easier.
